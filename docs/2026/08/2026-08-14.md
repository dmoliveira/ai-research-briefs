<h2 id="executive-summary">Executive Summary</h2>

This week's strongest work is about making agent performance attributable to the system around the model. <em>AI4AI at Test-Time</em> shows that a strong builder model can design inference harnesses that nearly double weaker target-model performance on Theory-of-Mind benchmarks without updating weights. IBM's <em>VAKRA</em> turns enterprise-style API-plus-document workflows into an executable benchmark with 8,000+ local APIs, trajectory replay, and tool-policy checks. <em>Retry, Switch, or Abstain?</em> makes tool failure a first-class training and evaluation variable rather than an exception path.

The skill story is getting sharper and more uncomfortable. <em>Agent Skills Can Be Harmful</em> attributes 307 failures or cost regressions to loaded skills and shows that relevant-looking skills can still cause omissions, overbuilding, and excessive verification. <em>Harness-IF</em> measures whether coding agents follow rules across deployed instruction surfaces, separating real compliance from actions the agent would have taken anyway. <em>Better, Faster, Stronger</em> argues that programmatic skills are the best path to reducing agent cost because deterministic procedures replace repeated trial and error.

On LLM reliability, Google Research's knowledge-profiling work reframes many factual errors as recall failures: frontier models may encode a fact but fail to retrieve it in the queried form. <em>Information Abundance Paradox</em> adds a complementary warning that long-context training can reduce pressure to internalize facts parametrically. For retrieval and scientific use, <em>QV-PIC</em> is the most practical systems paper, while <em>TRACES</em> is the most important cautionary benchmark: scientific agents often engage flawed premises rather than reject them.

The recommender-system signal is narrower but strong. <em>HCGRec</em> attacks a concrete optimization failure in semantic-ID generative recommenders by hinting only the hard branches that produce zero-advantage rollouts. <em>PRISM</em>, accepted at CIKM 2026, shows that self-attention in sequential recommendation can over-favor similar items and miss heterogeneous item relations that carry preference signal.

<h2 id="key-trends">Key Trends</h2>

<div class="brief-trend-grid">
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Harnesses are a transfer mechanism.</strong></p><p>Capability can move through deterministic code, routing, validation, and answer constraints, not only through distillation or fine-tuning.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Agent benchmarks are becoming executable.</strong></p><p>VAKRA, CTBench, Harness-IF, and TRACES all score process, evidence, or operating constraints instead of treating the final answer as enough.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Tool robustness needs explicit failure taxonomies.</strong></p><p>Retrying, switching, and abstaining are different policies; training on only clean tool calls hides deployment risk.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Skills are both assets and liabilities.</strong></p><p>Programmatic skills can reduce cost, but natural-language skill bundles can also induce functional failures, excessive procedures, and misplaced priorities.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Factuality is shifting from storage to access.</strong></p><p>Google's profiling work and the long-context paradox both suggest that where knowledge lives and how it is elicited now matter as much as whether it exists.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>RAG optimization is moving below the prompt layer.</strong></p><p>Serving work is now optimizing KV reuse, visual tokenization, query-aware resolution, and cache quality under real latency constraints.</p></div>
</div>

    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Link</span><span><a class="entry-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12307">https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12307</a></span></div>

  </div>
  <div class="entry-section-grid">
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Summary</span>The paper studies strong-to-weak scaffolding: a capable builder model iteratively designs a harness that helps a weaker target solve tasks more reliably at inference time.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Problem investigated</span>Distillation usually transfers capability by changing weights, but many deployment gains may come from better process, routing, checks, and answer constraints.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Context</span>Coding agents and tool-using systems already show that model behavior depends heavily on harness design.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Solutions explored</span>Builder models use 5% of benchmark data as validation, refine harnesses over multiple rounds, and then evaluate finalized harnesses on full test sets.</p></div>

    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Conclusion</span>Inference harnesses can transfer cognitive structure from strong models to weaker models without retraining.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">My take</span>This is a clean argument for treating harness design as a first-class ML artifact with validation data, ablations, and regression tests.</p></div>

    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Summary</span>BENCH2ROBUST injects transient, persistent, and silent tool failures into existing tool-use benchmarks and evaluates whether agents retry, switch tools, or stop.</p></div>
