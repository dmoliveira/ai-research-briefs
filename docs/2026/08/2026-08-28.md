<h2 id="executive-summary">Executive Summary</h2>

This week's best agent papers are less about broad capability claims and more about control surfaces. <em>Auto-Policy, not Auto-Skill</em> argues that high-risk skills need typed authority checks attached to the skill artifact, not only advisory instructions. <em>Tunable Tool-Call Rates</em> shows that a single residual-stream direction can move an instruction-tuned model along a tool-use cost/accuracy frontier without changing prompts or retraining. <em>LocalLSTC</em> and <em>Repair or Resample?</em> both make trajectory state explicit: one for local GUI agents, the other for reproducible multi-agent failure repair.

Evaluation work is getting sharper. <em>FrontierChallenge</em> and <em>BixBench3</em> evaluate scientific agents on complete workflow delivery, not isolated answers. Both expose a gap between partial progress and finished artifacts. <em>Trace Integrity</em> names the same failure mode for data agents: an answer can be correct while the computation behind it is invalid, unreplayable, or schema-unfaithful.

Retrieval and inference papers are strongly product-shaped. <em>Data Citation for Large Language Models</em> clarifies why provenance for datasets, subsets, query results, and graph facts is harder than ordinary document citation; <em>LivingRAG</em> adds reusable verified experience to Graph RAG; <em>ProgRouter</em> routes across multi-agent workflow steps based on estimated progress; and <em>AsymSpec</em> makes speculative decoding useful when the drafter and verifier see different context budgets.

The recommender and product-retrieval signal is unusually industrial. <em>An Event is Worth One Token</em> treats behavior-event tokenization as a scaling primitive for LLM recommendation. <em>TransRetrieval</em> pushes transformer retrieval into industrial candidate generation, while <em>CRAMER</em> frames controllable recommender editing as request-aware masking rather than full model rewrites.

<h2 id="key-trends">Key Trends</h2>

<div class="brief-trend-grid">
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Agent behavior is becoming a tunable systems variable.</strong></p><p>Tool-call steering, online model routing, and temporal GUI control all expose knobs that product teams can measure against cost, latency, and side-effect risk.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Trajectory replay is replacing anecdotal debugging.</strong></p><p>SymTrace-style intervention anchors and trace-integrity contracts make agent failures reproducible enough to compare repair methods.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Scientific-agent benchmarks are raising the completion bar.</strong></p><p>FrontierChallenge and BixBench3 grade complete deliverables and derived artifacts, revealing large gaps behind confident completion claims.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>RAG systems are accumulating state.</strong></p><p>Reusable query experience and data-level provenance shift retrieval systems from one-shot evidence lookup toward maintained, auditable knowledge substrates.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>LLM judges need supply-side diagnostics.</strong></p><p>Candidate-pool analysis shows that judging quality depends on whether correct answers are present, frequent, or merely recognizable.</p></div>
  <div class="brief-trend-card"><p><strong>Industrial recommenders are standardizing LLM-scale inputs.</strong></p><p>Fresh recommendation papers focus on event tokens, transformer retrieval, and controlled editing rather than only larger rerankers.</p></div>
</div>

  <h3>Auto-Policy, not Auto-Skill: Compiled Agent Skills for the Physical World</h3>
  <p class="entry-summary">A typed authority layer for high-risk agent skills that checks world-state and sensor evidence before physical actions execute.</p>
  <div class="entry-meta-grid">
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Published / updated</span><span>2026-08-25 / 2026-08-25; arXiv ID 2608.25091</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Authors</span><span>Zhonghao Zhan, Hamed Haddadi</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Affiliations</span><span>Not listed in arXiv listing metadata</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Link</span><span><a class="entry-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25091">https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25091</a></span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Tags</span><span>agent skills, physical-world agents, authorization, safety, tool governance</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Reading profile</span><span>25-40 min - Relevance 5/5 - Quality 4/5</span></div>
  </div>
  <div class="entry-section-grid">
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Summary</span>The paper argues that skills describe behavior but do not encode whether an agent is authorized to turn that behavior into a real-world action.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Problem investigated</span>Automatically generated or shared skills can carry borrowed authority, where one agent's procedural instruction causes another agent to perform physical or high-risk actions without typed permission checks.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Context</span>Skill artifacts are becoming portable agent deployment units, but physical-world tasks such as locks, devices, payments, and robotics need stronger guardrails than markdown instructions.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Solutions explored</span>Edge Skillguard packages typed guards inside the skill artifact, checking world state and sensor evidence before invocation rather than relying on downstream workflow engines alone.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Key results</span>On a live edge control-plane testbed, guards reject 60/60 borrowed-authority requests across five attack variants without blocking benign requests, and results hold at 5x scale across hosts over a Tailscale mesh.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Conclusion</span>High-risk agent skills should co-package procedural knowledge with machine-checkable invocation policy.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">My take</span>This is a useful corrective to auto-skill enthusiasm: more reusable procedures are not safer unless authority travels in a typed, enforceable form.</p></div>
  </div>
</article>

  <h3>Tunable Tool-Call Rates in LLM Agents via Representation Steering</h3>
  <p class="entry-summary">An inference-time intervention that raises or lowers tool-call propensity through one residual-stream direction.</p>
  <div class="entry-meta-grid">
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Published / updated</span><span>2026-08-25 / 2026-08-25; arXiv ID 2608.25198</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Authors</span><span>Yuqi Chen, Vincent Siu, Yang Liu, Dawn Song, Chenguang Wang</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Affiliations</span><span>Not listed in arXiv listing metadata</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Link</span><span><a class="entry-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25198">https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25198</a></span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Tags</span><span>tool use, representation steering, agents, inference-time control, cost-quality tradeoffs</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Reading profile</span><span>30-45 min - Relevance 5/5 - Quality 5/5</span></div>
  </div>
  <div class="entry-section-grid">
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Summary</span>The authors extract a linear tool-use direction from an instruction-tuned model's own preference signal and scale it during inference to control call frequency.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Problem investigated</span>Agents over-call tools when they do not need them and under-call tools when answers require external state, creating cost, latency, and correctness failures.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Context</span>Prompt tuning and post-training are slow levers for behavior that often needs deployment-time adjustment per domain or cost envelope.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Solutions explored</span>The method adds or subtracts a residual-stream direction with strength alpha, tests generalization to unseen tools, and sweeps live tool execution to trace a Pareto frontier.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Key results</span>Call rates move monotonically from near 0% to over 90% while calls remain well formed; a live sweep nearly doubles open-domain QA accuracy from 0.29 to 0.56 and transfers across dense, MoE, and multimodal models.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Conclusion</span>Tool-use propensity can be exposed as a lightweight inference-time control axis.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">My take</span>This is the issue's most practical runtime paper: production agents need adjustable tool budgets, not only better default prompts.</p></div>
  </div>
</article>

  <div class="entry-meta-grid">
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Published / updated</span><span>2026-08-26 / 2026-08-26; arXiv ID 2608.25777</span></div>

    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Link</span><span><a class="entry-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25777">https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25777</a></span></div>

  </div>
  <div class="entry-section-grid">
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Summary</span>LocalLSTC keeps active subgoal, evidence, and feedback in persistent long-term control while short-term execution makes bounded step commitments.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Problem investigated</span>Local GUI agents lose track of task stage, accumulated evidence, and recovery state when all control information is implicit in a growing trajectory.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Context</span>Teams want locally deployed GUI agents for privacy and cost, but smaller local backbones are especially sensitive to state reconstruction failures.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Solutions explored</span>The architecture separates Long-Term Control, Short-Term Execution, Long-to-Short Planning, and Short-to-Long Control without additional training.</p></div>

    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Conclusion</span>Temporal organization of control information is an architecture dimension for GUI agents, not just a prompt-memory detail.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">My take</span>The useful lesson is portable: make progress state explicit and durable, especially when the model is not strong enough to re-infer it every step.</p></div>

  <h3>Repair or Resample? Rethinking Failure Debugging in LLM Multi-Agent Systems</h3>
  <p class="entry-summary">A controlled replay framework for separating causal multi-agent repair from lucky stochastic reruns.</p>
  <div class="entry-meta-grid">
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Published / updated</span><span>2026-08-26 / 2026-08-26; arXiv ID 2608.25920</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Authors</span><span>Zhongwen Luan, Xiaoyu Zhang, Ming Hu, Yue Yang, Jiongchi Yu, Xiaohong Chen</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Affiliations</span><span>Not listed in arXiv listing metadata</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Link</span><span><a class="entry-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25920">https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25920</a></span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Tags</span><span>multi-agent systems, debugging, replay, failure repair, software engineering</span></div>
    <div class="entry-meta-item"><span class="entry-meta-label">Reading profile</span><span>35-50 min - Relevance 5/5 - Quality 5/5</span></div>
  </div>
  <div class="entry-section-grid">
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Summary</span>SymTrace reconstructs execution before intervention anchors and only regenerates downstream behavior, enabling controlled tests of repair methods on the SymFail dataset.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Problem investigated</span>Many multi-agent debugging methods rerun whole trajectories, making it unclear whether a method repaired a cause or just sampled a better path.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Context</span>Multi-agent coding, reasoning, and workflow systems are increasingly evaluated with noisy trajectories whose failures are hard to reproduce.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Solutions explored</span>The authors introduce replay anchors, graph-linked failure labels, trace evidence, and symptom-driven interventions across three mainstream MAS frameworks.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Key results</span>Unguided rerun methods show only 67.97% failure reproduction and 6.90% repair rates; symptom-driven intervention repairs 20.15% of failed cases, a 191.89% improvement over the tested repair methods.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Conclusion</span>Multi-agent repair should be measured through controlled replay and localized interventions rather than raw rerun success.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">My take</span>This is a necessary benchmark pattern for agent ops: without replay control, repair claims are mostly confounded by sampling variance.</p></div>
  </div>
</article>

  <div class="entry-section-grid">
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Summary</span>The paper isolates candidate generation, peer communication, and terminal selection to ask when an LLM judge rescues correct answers already present in a pool.</p></div>
    <div class="entry-section"><p><span class="entry-section-title">Problem investigated</span>Multi-agent systems may generate a correct answer but still converge on a popular wrong answer, while evaluations often change several system components at once.</p></div>
